The prototype is four facilities, one supplier, four delivery zones, 15 products and 2,000 parcels a day of demand. Every process runs at 100,000 EA/day for $1, no work centre has a capacity, and the transport costs are ordinal placeholders (9 / 99 / 999 from a Dummy node). It is a syntax demonstrator and should be read as one — the value is in the shapes, not the numbers.

Section 1

What the prototype actually does

Only one path is switched on by default. Six recipes are set to Exclude — those are the scenario levers. Reading the enabled set end to end gives the baseline three-sort chain; the excluded set gives the deep-sort and local-sort variants.

The two mechanics worth stealing

Sort depth lives in the product name. Unload_Parcel_2_L3_Sort means "unloaded at site 2, already sorted to level 3". The L1/L2/L3 tag is how the model remembers what a downstream site still has to do — which is precisely the tracking question they raised, and precisely what our Despatch1_<hub> tag does today.

A production policy with no work centre is a free conversion. The row Sort-Hub · Unload_Parcel_2_L3_Sort → Outbound-Parcel_2 has no work centre and no process, so it renames a deep-sorted parcel into a despatchable one without consuming sortation capacity. That is a clean way to model cross-dock: the parcel still pays unload and reload, it just doesn't pay the sorter. This is the single most useful pattern in the file for us.

But note what that means

A deep-sorted parcel in this prototype still stops at the Sort-Hub — it pays SortHub_Unload_LR on the way in and a reload on the way out. Deep sort here saves the sort, not the stop. Skipping the building entirely is a different lane, and that lane is broken (finding 1 below). We should agree which of the two "bypass" means before we build it.

Question 1

Source/sink linkage across chain 1 and chain 2

Their answer — that different sortation depths need additional intermediate products, and that the count grows with the number of lanes those states can flow through — is correct, and it lands on the one place our model is genuinely brittle. Here is the state of our own build, measured rather than remembered.

The two chains do not touch

Our final model has 226 products. Of those, 72 are terminal — they are never a component of any BOM — and every *_Despatch1_* flavour is in that set. Another 65 are source-only: they are never produced by any recipe and are injected at suppliers instead, including all STG_*_Despatch2, VIC_* and INTERSTATE_* states.

That is the two-entity architecture working as designed: chain 1 (pickup) ends at Despatch1, chain 2 (delivery) begins from observed interstate + stage supply, and the two meet only where they compete for the same work centres and facility capacity. It keeps chain 2 honest against the scan data. But it means there is no arc from a parcel collected to a parcel delivered.

The flexibility gap

Any scenario whose answer depends on collection volume reaching delivery cannot be asked of the model as it stands. Close a PUD and the delivery side does not move. Shift 60/40 pickup between hubs and the terminating side does not notice. Consolidate four PUDs and you get a first-mile answer and a last-mile answer that were computed independently and happen to share a building.

This is a deliberate trade — we chose observed chain 2 over conserved flow, and it was the right call for calibration. But before scenario modelling starts we need to decide which of these we are doing, because they need different tables:

  • Keep them separate and treat every scenario result as two answers that share capacity. Cheapest, and defensible for footprint and activity questions. Not defensible for "what does this do to a parcel's journey".
  • Bridge them — make chain 1's Despatch1 output a component of chain 2's round-2 recipes, and cut chain 2's supply by whatever chain 1 delivers. This is the full-conservation model. It costs us the direct scan calibration on the VIC-origin band and it re-opens the origin-mix fitting.
  • Bridge only the VIC band — the 49.0% of delivery volume that is Victorian-origin is, physically, chain 1's output. Interstate (33.2%) and stage (17.8%) stay as observed supply. This preserves the calibration where the scans are authoritative and closes the loop where it actually matters. This is the option I'd put forward.

Tables impacted

Adding sortation states touches more than the obvious three. From the prototype's own shape, a new parcel state costs us a row in each of: Products, BillsOfMaterials, ProductionPolicies (one per competing work centre — so ×3 for unload, ×3 for load), Groups, TransportationPolicies (per lane it may travel), and, if it is pinned, FlowConstraints. Our current ratio is 226 products to 20,790 transport rows across 1,595 origin-destination pairs — roughly 92 lane rows per product. That multiplier is the thing to watch.

Question 2

Work-centre flow allocation — this one we should adopt

Their construct is four UserDefinedVariables rows and two UserDefinedConstraints rows. Written out, the long-reach one is:

IN-HUB_UnloadingSplit_LR
  term  TotalInboundVolume   coeff -0.5   Production   process-group grp_in-Hub_Unload   Aggregate
  term  Total_Unloading_LR   coeff +1.0   Production   process       HUB_Unload_LR       Enumerate

HubUnloading_Forced_LR   ->   Fixed With Tolerance = 0

i.e.   1 x LR  -  0.5 x (LR + Other)  =  0     ->     LR = 50% of total unloading

The algebra is right and the Aggregate / Enumerate pairing is the interesting part: Aggregate on the process group sums both unload methods into one total; Enumerate on the single process picks out the numerator. That is a genuinely reusable pattern.

Why we need it

Our model has no UserDefinedVariables or UserDefinedConstraints tables at all. Today the unload mix is decided by cost and capacity alone — three competing recipes per product, priced on the process:

Melbourne Parcel · unloadUnit costCapacity EA/dayUsed, last WC runShare
UNLOAD_ULD$0.015129,65268,19888.7%
UNLOAD_LONGREACH$0.02082,5058,70211.3%
UNLOAD_HAND$0.05035,35900.0%

Hand unload is exactly zero at both hubs, because it is the dearest recipe and there is nothing forcing it. The optimiser fills the cheapest method until something stops it. So the split we report is not an assumption we made — it is a by-product of capacities that were themselves sized from an assumed split. Circular, and it understates hand-unload labour to nil. A user-defined constraint replaces that with a number we can state, defend and vary.

Three things to raise before we copy it
  • Don't pin it hard. Fixed With Tolerance = 0 is an equality. If the pinned method runs out of capacity the model goes infeasible rather than spilling — and we have been bitten by exactly this before, where a Conditional Min above 1,323 EA made the build infeasible. Ask for the Min/Max band form (two constraints, ≥ lower and ≤ upper) so a share can flex under pressure and we can see where it wants to go.
  • Scope it below the facility total. Pinning 50% of all unloading at a site lets the optimiser satisfy the ratio with whichever volume is cheapest to move — it could route ULD-arriving linehaul to hand unload. The mix is really a property of how the vehicle arrives. The UserDefinedVariables table has productname and modename columns; we should scope by inbound mode or product family, not facility total.
  • Confirm the group trick scales. If facilitynamegroupbehavior = Enumerate against a facility group generates one constraint per facility, we can express the whole eight-site, three-method mix in a handful of rows instead of 24. If it doesn't, this becomes 24 hand-maintained rows and we need the generator to emit them.

Two smaller notes: with only two methods, constraining both to 0.5 is redundant — the second is implied — and if the shares ever fail to sum to 1 the pair is infeasible with no diagnostic. And Fixed With Tolerance is used with no tolerance visible in the table; worth asking where the tolerance is set.

Question 3

Deep sort and the tentacle constraint — still open

This was the question we most needed answered, and it is the one the model does not demonstrate. Their reply says "flow count / production count constraints will be able to limit the number of sortation buckets". Searched across all 17 tables: there is no flow-count constraint, no production-count constraint, and the one FlowConstraints row present is a Conditional Min that is Excluded. The capability is asserted, not shown.

And the prototype can't show it as built

The reason is structural rather than an oversight. A tentacle constraint limits how many distinct destinations a facility can sort to — 12 delivery PDCs is a count of chutes, not a volume. For a solver to count destinations, the destination has to be visible in the model, which means the sorted product has to be flavoured by where it is going.

In this prototype it isn't. Outbound-Parcel_3 is one product serving all four delivery zones. There is nothing for a count constraint to count. The moment a destination tag is added, the product set multiplies — which is the cost they correctly warned us about, and it is not small for us:

StageProducts todayFlavoured byIf flavoured by 12 PDCs
Round-1 despatch (Despatch1)448 sort sites (sparse)~216
Round-2 despatch (Despatch2)3811 depots + VIC/INT~38 (already destination-flavoured)

18 distinct upstream states × 12 PDC destinations gives ~216 round-1 despatch products against 44 today, taking the model to roughly 400 products and — at our current ~92 lane rows per product — a proportionate jump in TransportationPolicies. Manageable, since it is all generated, but it needs to be a decision rather than a surprise.

The naming problem underneath it

Their state names encode position in the chain: Unload_Parcel_1_… is the hub, _2_ the sort hub, _3_ the local depot. That works for a fixed three-tier chain. It breaks on the exact scenario they are proposing — excess deep sort spilling from Melbourne Parcel to another automated hub — because that second hub is site 2 for the spilled flow and site 1 for its own. The index collides.

Our model already avoids this: Despatch1_<hub> tags the parcel with which site sorted it, and the round-2 recipe at each hub withholds its own flavour, so a parcel structurally cannot be sorted twice at the same building. That role-agnostic tag is what makes a spill-to-a-peer-hub scenario expressible. Worth putting to them as the pattern to standardise on, rather than positional numbering.

What to ask for
  • A worked example of the count constraint itself — a facility that can sort to N destinations and no more, with the excess spilling to a peer. Which constraint type, which table, and does it need binaries.
  • Whether the count is over destinations or over products. If the only mechanism counts distinct products flowing on a lane, then "12 chutes" has to be encoded as 12 destination-flavoured products and the constraint counts those — which is a different modelling contract and needs confirming before we generate 400 products on the assumption.
  • How the spill is priced. Today deep sort is $3 vs regular $1 — ordinal placeholders. The real question is whether the avoided second sort plus the avoided handling beats the extra linehaul, and that needs our activity costs in their structure.
  • Whether "bypass" means skipping the sort or skipping the building. The ByPass transport mode in the file suggests the latter; the free-conversion recipe delivers the former. They are different scenarios with different savings.
Section 5

On their general observations

Pre-computed % columns driven by scenario items. Agree, and it is the right way to keep the scenario count down. The catch is ownership: our tables are generated from notebooks and regenerated often, so any custom column added by hand inside Cosmic Frog is lost on the next upload. If we go this way, the generator has to emit the custom columns, and we need to agree the column names up front as a contract.

Troubleshooting across product states. Agree, and worth putting real time into on the next call. Two things that would help concretely: a build-time validator that walks the recipe graph and reports any product that cannot reach demand or cannot be produced — running that over their prototype is what surfaced findings 1 and 2 in about a second — and a naming convention where the state tag is parseable, so a flow report can be grouped by stage without a lookup table.

Include/Exclude as the scenario mechanism. The prototype shows why the validator matters. Deep sort is not one toggle; it is at least four rows that have to flip together, and flipping three of four gives a model that solves but quietly routes everything the long way. Scenario definitions need to be named sets with a check that the set is complete.
